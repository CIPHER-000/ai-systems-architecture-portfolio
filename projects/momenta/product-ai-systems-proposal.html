Section 1Where Momenta Is, and Why This Moment Matters

Momenta has done something rare. You validated demand by hand, ran the business through WhatsApp, and crossed $250k in GMV before writing serious product code.

That sequence matters. The product roadmap is not theoretical, it is grounded in real conversations with real customers who paid for experiences you personally curated.

What stood out in our conversation is that you are not trying to build another marketplace. Airbnb already solved supply liquidity. Momenta is solving something different: people do not lack experiences to choose from, they lack a trusted way to find the right one for the right moment with the right people.

Curation, trust, and emotional context. Whatever we build should reinforce those three, not dilute them.

Section 2The Real Product Opportunity

A small personal note before getting into this. A friend and I recently spent close to two weeks trying to figure out a single place to hang out.

We just wanted somewhere calm, conversational, good ambience, decent food. We bounced between TikTok saves, YouTube videos, Google Maps reviews, and asking people in group chats. We had more information than we could use and still could not decide. That is the exact problem Momenta is solving.

The problem is not lack of information. It is fragmented contextual discovery.

A few things become clearer once you sit with that:

  • Discovery is contextual, not informational. People are not searching for "rooftop bar in Lagos." They are trying to figure out where to take four friends visiting from out of town on a Friday when one of them does not drink.
  • Group bookings are the default. When 95% of bookings involve groups and the average party size is around four, the product is fundamentally social. The AI's job is not optimizing for one person's taste, it is finding the overlap across a small group.
  • Personalization compounds. The more the system understands a user's rhythm, the better every recommendation gets. That compounding is what quietly turns into a long-term advantage.
Search engines optimize for finding. Momenta should optimize for deciding.

Section 3The AI Concierge

The concierge should feel like texting a friend who happens to know every great spot in town and remembers what you and your group are into.

Not a chatbot. Not a search bar with personality. A trusted, lightweight presence.

A few principles:

  • Conversation over forms. Users should never feel like they are filling out a preference survey. The system learns through natural exchanges.
  • Fewer, better suggestions. Two or three thoughtful recommendations beat a list of twenty. Decision fatigue kills bookings.
  • Explain the why. A recommendation without context feels random. A recommendation with a sentence about why it fits ("quieter spot, good for a smaller group, kitchen actually stays open late on Sundays") feels human.
  • Hold context across the group. When someone mentions "we have a friend visiting" or "one of us is vegetarian," that should shape the rest of the conversation without anyone repeating it.
  • Know when to step back. If a user is browsing, let them browse. If they want a suggestion, give them one. The product should feel calm.

The tone matters as much as the logic. Warm, minimal, lightly opinionated. Closer to a good concierge at a small hotel than a customer service bot.

Section 4Behavioral Memory

The system should quietly build a picture of each user over time without ever feeling intrusive.

Things worth learning:

  • Preferred ambience (loud, intimate, outdoors, lounge-y)
  • Typical group size and composition
  • Activity rhythm (weekday dinners, weekend brunches, late-night, daytime)
  • Price comfort range, inferred from actual bookings rather than asked
  • Recurring companions and how preferences shift when they are present
  • Cuisines, music, and themes that come up repeatedly
  • What they declined and why

None of this should require onboarding flows or repeated questions. Memory grows through booking history, conversational signals, and lightweight feedback after experiences. Over time the concierge stops needing to ask and starts knowing.

This is also where the product gets better with age. Most apps feel a little worse over time. A behavioral system goes the other way. The more someone uses it, the more it understands them, and the more useful each suggestion gets. Users do not need to be told this is happening. They just notice that Momenta keeps getting the call right, and they start coming back for the bigger decisions instead of the small ones.

Section 5Recommendation Philosophy

Most recommendation systems are built around maximizing options. Momenta should do the opposite.

  • Curated outputs, not feeds. The user is not scrolling. They are choosing.
  • Quality over coverage. Better to say "I don't have a great match for that this weekend" than to recommend something mediocre. Honesty builds trust faster than completeness.
  • Context-shaped, not query-shaped. The same user asking the same question on a Tuesday afternoon versus a Saturday night should get different answers.
  • Recommendations should feel intentional. Every suggestion is a small statement of taste. That is what makes the brand.

Section 6Supply Intelligence

This is the part most platforms skip, and it is what would make Momenta genuinely defensible.

The concierge is only as good as what it can recommend. Generic internet data is noisy and unranked. Momenta needs its own internal source of truth for what is actually good, and that requires real operational intelligence on the supply side.

What this looks like in practice:

  • Discovery. Continuously surface new venues, hosts, and experiences from Instagram, TikTok, reviews, local press, and tip lines.
  • Enrichment. Pull together photos, menus, vibe signals, operating realities (is the kitchen actually open when they say it is), and social proof.
  • Scoring. Rank candidates on signals that map to Momenta's standards: aesthetic quality, consistency, host responsiveness, group-friendliness, uniqueness.
  • AI-assisted curation with humans in the loop. Early on, the AI does the heavy filtering while the team makes the final call. Over time, high-confidence, low-risk candidates can move through AI-assisted or automated approval, with auditability and human override always available. That keeps the brand intact while removing the bottleneck of manual scouting.
  • Feedback loop. Booking outcomes and post-experience signals feed back into supply scoring, so the system gets sharper over time.

The user never sees this layer. They only see the result, which is that every recommendation feels considered.

The two systems reinforce each other. Better supply data sharpens the concierge. Better concierge data sharpens supply scoring. That loop is the long-term advantage.

Section 7Technical Direction

Keeping this high level on purpose. The goal is to sketch the shape, not over-specify.

At the product layer

  • A conversational interface that handles natural multi-turn exchanges
  • A behavioral memory store tied to each user and, where relevant, each group
  • A curated supply database that the recommendation layer pulls from
  • A recommendation engine that blends behavioral context, group context, and supply quality
  • An internal scoring and enrichment pipeline for new supply
  • Lightweight retrieval so the concierge can quickly pull relevant context into a conversation

Two layers worth separating mentally

  1. A deterministic curation layer. The source of truth: vetted supply, structured signals, scoring. Auditable and editable by the team.
  2. A conversational layer. What the user touches. It explains, personalizes, and adapts, but it pulls from the curated layer rather than improvising from the open internet.

That separation is what keeps the product trustworthy.

The AI does not hallucinate restaurants. It speaks fluently about a curated set.

Review of the Current Stack

You asked for a candid take on whether the current stack aligns with where the product is going. Short answer: yes. It is a strong startup foundation. A few thoughts on how it evolves naturally as the AI side matures.

What is working well

  • NestJS, PostgreSQL, Redis, Bull/BullMQ, Next.js, Prisma, Docker, Cloudflare, BetterStack/Sentry is a pragmatic, modern stack. Good iteration velocity, sensible operational footprint, easy to hire into.
  • WebSockets in the architecture are a real asset. Conversational AI, live recommendations, and group planning all benefit from persistent connections. That choice ages well.
  • PostgreSQL is a particularly strong foundation. Behavioral memory, booking history, group context, and conversational state all map cleanly to a relational model. A lot of teams over-rotate to specialized databases too early. Staying on Postgres is the right call.
  • Prisma is a strong velocity choice early on. As recommendation and retrieval workloads grow more complex, some of the heavier memory and ranking queries may benefit from more direct retrieval paths. Not a problem today, just something to keep an eye on.

Where the stack will likely evolve

  • Semantic retrieval and vector memory. The current setup does not yet appear to include embeddings or vector search. As the concierge gets more personal, semantic memory becomes increasingly important. The most natural path is pgvector inside the existing Postgres instance, which keeps the operational surface area small and avoids pulling in a separate vector database.
  • Search ranking. Meilisearch is a solid keyword search engine. Over time, contextual and semantic ranking will start to matter more than keyword match, especially for the concierge experience. That shift can happen gradually alongside the embeddings work.
  • Background AI pipelines. Supply intelligence work like scraping, enrichment, scoring, and embedding generation will eventually want dedicated background workers. BullMQ is already in place, which makes this a natural extension rather than a new system.
  • Infrastructure. The current direction feels right-sized. As AI orchestration and background workloads grow, infrastructure can evolve alongside product complexity. No need to over-build now.
The summary is that the foundation is in good shape. The evolution is mostly additive, not corrective.

Section 8Execution Approach

A few things worth committing to as we build:

  • MVP-first on the concierge. Get a small, opinionated version in front of real users quickly. Even a basic version that returns two thoughtful suggestions per query will teach us more than months of design work.
  • Behavioral signals from day one. Even before the AI is sophisticated, log booking patterns, conversation context, and post-experience feedback. The data compounds.
  • Phase supply intelligence behind the concierge. Start with semi-automated curation supporting the team. Layer in automation as patterns emerge.
  • Resist sophistication for its own sake. The product wins on warmth and quality of recommendation, not on AI complexity. Every model and feature should earn its place by improving the user's actual experience.
  • Iterate on tone constantly. The voice of the concierge is a product surface. It deserves the same care as visual design.

Section 9Team Structure

Momenta's next phase spans three important areas: AI and backend systems, frontend and mobile product, and UX/UI design. These are connected, but they should not be treated as one vague responsibility.

The AI and backend layer can lead the foundation early on: concierge logic, behavioral memory, supply intelligence, recommendation systems, integrations, and the technical architecture underneath all of it. I can own this end of the product directly.

Frontend and mobile ownership matters because the concierge experience has to feel simple, fast, and natural for users, especially as the product moves toward mobile. It is not a thin layer on top of an API. It is most of what the user actually feels.

UX/UI ownership matters because Momenta's brand leans heavily on warmth, trust, and simplicity. The product cannot feel like a generic AI chatbot or a typical marketplace. Holding that line consistently takes a dedicated owner.

The practical recommendation is to start lean, but with clear ownership across three areas:

  • AI, backend, and systems direction Concierge, memory, supply intelligence, recommendation pipelines, and technical architecture.
  • Frontend and mobile execution The conversational interface, mobile experience, and how the product actually feels in a user's hand.
  • UX/UI and brand experience The visual and interaction language that keeps Momenta calm, premium, and clearly itself.

This is not about adding headcount early. It is about avoiding a situation where one person is expected to carry every layer of the product at the same depth. Clear separation of concerns will help Momenta move faster without sacrificing product quality.

Section 10Closing Thoughts

The hard part at Momenta is already done. You proved people will pay, you proved they want curation, and you proved the brand can carry trust.

What comes next is mostly about translating that judgment into a product layer that scales without losing the warmth that got you here.

The opportunity I keep coming back to is the behavioral one. Every conversation, every booking, every "we loved it" or "not really our vibe" makes the concierge a little smarter and a little more useful for the next decision. That kind of compounding trust is genuinely rare in consumer products, and it is the thing that turns a good experience into one people quietly rely on.

If we get this right, finding something to do with the people you care about stops feeling like a research project and more like asking a friend who just happens to know. That is a product I would personally use, which is honestly the best signal I have. Excited to keep building toward it with you.
